You're fighting a five-pound smallmouth on a windblown point, the drag is singing, and then the handle suddenly grinds like it's full of sand. The trick is knowing which ones actually deliver sealed drags, smooth gearing, and bodies that won't flex under load — and which ones are just shiny marketing. That's what this guide sorts out.

## What to look for

Before you start comparing spec sheets, focus on the handful of features that genuinely separate a $200 reel from a $90 one.

- **Drag system and sealing.** A smooth, sealed front drag keeps water and grit out and delivers consistent pressure. Carbon-fiber drag washers are the current standard in this price band and resist the stick-slip that causes line to snap on a hard run.
- **Body material and weight.** Aluminum or carbon-composite bodies hold up better than graphite and transfer less flex into the gear train. A lighter reel balances a rod better and reduces fatigue on long days, but don't chase weight at the cost of durability.
- **Gear quality and bearing count.** More bearings isn't automatically better, but a machined main gear and quality bearings (often stainless or corrosion-resistant) make a real difference in smoothness and longevity. Look for reels rated for both fresh and saltwater if you fish the coast.
- **Line capacity and retrieve ratio.** Match the reel's spool to the line and techniques you actually use. A higher gear ratio (around 6.2:1 and up) helps with fast retrieves and working lures, while a lower ratio gives more torque for cranking.

## FAQ

**Is a $200 spinning reel really better than a $100 one?**
In most cases, yes — but the gap is smaller than the price suggests. The extra money typically buys better sealing, a stiffer body, smoother drag washers, and higher-quality bearings. If you fish hard, in saltwater, or target bigger fish, that upgrade pays off. If you fish casually in freshwater, a good $90 reel like the Pflueger President XT will serve you well.

**What size spinning reel should I get for bass fishing?**
For most bass applications, a 2500 or 3000 size reel is the sweet spot. It balances well on a medium or medium-heavy rod, holds enough line for long casts, and has the drag strength to handle bass in cover. If you're fishing ultralight finesse setups, a 1000 or 2000 size works fine.

**Do I need a sealed reel if I only fish freshwater?**
Not strictly, but sealing still helps. A sealed drag keeps grit and water out, which extends the life of the reel and keeps drag performance consistent. If you fish from a boat in the rain or wade fish, sealing becomes more valuable. For pure fair-weather freshwater fishing, it's a nice-to-have rather than a must.

**How often should I service a spinning reel?**
For freshwater use, a light cleaning and re-oiling once or twice a season is usually enough. If you fish saltwater, rinse the reel with fresh water after every trip and plan on a more thorough service at least once a year. Reels that see heavy salt exposure may need servicing more often to prevent corrosion.

**Can I use a freshwater spinning reel in saltwater?**
You can, but you need to be diligent about maintenance. Rinse the reel thoroughly with fresh water after every trip, avoid dunking it, and service it regularly. Reels designed specifically for saltwater, like the Penn Battle IV or Daiwa BG MQ, will hold up far better over time and are worth the extra investment if you fish the coast regularly.
