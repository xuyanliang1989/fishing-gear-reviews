You're on a dock at first light, tied to a schooling bass that just crushed a walking topwater, and your drag starts stuttering like a rusty screen door. Or maybe it's the opposite — the fish is barely peeling line and your reel handle suddenly binds up because a single drop of water got past a cheap seal. The sub-$100 spinning reel market in 2026 is genuinely stacked with legit gear from Shimano, Daiwa, Penn, Pflueger, Lew's, and Abu Garcia. I've fished most of these reels and watched plenty of them fail on the water, so let's talk about which ones actually earn their spot on your rod.

## Shimano Sedona FJ

**Price range: $79–$99**

The Sedona has been the default answer to "what's a good cheap spinning reel" for over a decade, and the FJ generation keeps that reputation intact. Shimano's HAGANE gear design — cold-forged rather than cut — is the real story here, because it's the same basic metallurgy philosophy they use up the lineup. That means the gear teeth stay meshed tighter and smoother over years of use instead of developing that gravelly grind you get from cheaper stamped gears.

**Pros:**
- The HAGANE cold-forged drive gear gives you noticeably smoother cranking under load than most reels in this price bracket, especially when you're winching a crankbait through grass.
- Shimano's Propulsion Line Management system (the angled spool lip) genuinely cuts down on wind knots and line slap, which matters a lot if you throw braid.
- It's light for the money — the 2500 size comes in around 7.6 oz — so it balances nicely on a 6'6" to 7' medium rod without making the combo tip-heavy.

**Con:**
- The drag is smooth but not especially strong or sealed, and the max drag numbers on the smaller sizes are modest — this is not the reel you want if you're regularly tangling with big redfish or stripers that make long runs.

**Real-world use:** I've used Sedonas for pond-hopping largemouth and smallmouth, and they handle everything from weightless Senkos to 3/8 oz spinnerbaits without complaint. It's also a great panfish and trout reel because the light rotor starts and stops cleanly on small lures. If you fish a few times a month and rinse your gear, a Sedona will last you several seasons.

**Best for:** The angler who wants one dependable all-around freshwater reel and doesn't want to think about it again for three years.

## Daiwa Revros LT

**Price range: $49–$69**

If the Sedona is the safe pick, the Revros LT is the value pick. Daiwa's LT (Light and Tough) concept shrank the body and rotor down while keeping the internals beefy, and the result is a reel that weighs less than a lot of reels costing twice as much. The Air Rotor design also makes the retrieve feel more precise on finesse presentations.

**Pros:**
- It's remarkably light — the 2500 size is right around 7.2 oz — which makes a huge difference if you're fishing drop shots or Ned rigs all day and your wrist is screaming by hour four.
- The ATD (Automatic Tournament Drag) is smooth and consistent on hooksets, and it does a good job protecting light line when a fish surges at the boat.
- At under $70 street price, you can buy two and still come in under the cost of one mid-tier reel, which matters if you're rigging multiple rods.

**Pros (yes, a third):**
- The machined aluminum spool and the overall fit and finish punch well above the price tag — it doesn't feel like a $50 reel in the hand.

**Con:**
- The body is mostly composite, and while it holds up fine for freshwater, it flexes a bit under heavy load, and saltwater guys will want to rinse it religiously or step up to something with better sealing.

**Real-world use:** This is my go-to recommendation for a kid's first "real" reel or a backup rod you keep in the truck. I've caught hundreds of pond bass on a Revros LT 2000 paired with 6 lb fluorocarbon, and the drag has never given me a surprise. It's also a legit crappie and bluegill reel because it handles 2 lb test without dumping line off the spool.

**Best for:** Budget-conscious anglers, beginners, and anyone who wants a light finesse reel without paying finesse-reel prices.

## Penn Pursuit IV

**Price range: $59–$79**

Penn built its name on saltwater reels, and the Pursuit IV is the entry point into that lineage. It's heavier than the Daiwa and Shimano options here, but it's also the most "salt-ready" reel in this price range, with a sealed drag system and corrosion-resistant bearings that won't seize up after a few trips in the surf.

**Pros:**
- The sealed drag is the standout feature — it keeps water and grit out, which is exactly what kills cheap reels in saltwater, and it means you can dunk it accidentally and keep fishing.
- The all-metal body and side plate give it a solid, no-flex feel when you're cranking hard against a fish, which is reassuring when a bluefish is trying to take your plug.
- The HT-100 drag washers are carbon fiber and deliver smooth, consistent pressure — no jerky lockup when a fish makes a sudden run.

**Con:**
- It's noticeably heavier than the freshwater-focused reels on this list (the 3000 size is around 10.5 oz), so it's not the best choice if you're throwing finesse baits all day and want a featherweight setup.

**Real-world use:** I keep a Pursuit IV 4000 spooled with 20 lb braid for inshore trips — redfish, speckled trout, flounder, and the occasional small tarpon. It's also a great catfish reel for bank fishing because it takes abuse and doesn't care. If you fish brackish water or hit the beach a few times a year, this is the reel that won't leave you stranded.

**Best for:** Inshore saltwater anglers and catfish fishermen who need a reel that shrugs off salt and grit.

## Pflueger President

**Price range: $59–$79**

The President is the reel that made Pflueger a household name in freshwater circles, and it's still one of the smoothest reels you can buy for under $80. The 10-bearing system and the braid-ready spool make it a favorite among bass and walleye anglers who want a refined feel without spending a lot.

**Pros:**
- Ten stainless steel ball bearings give it a silky retrieve that rivals reels costing $50 more — you feel it immediately when you crank the handle.
- The braid-ready aluminum spool has a rubberized band that grips braided line so it doesn't slip on the spool, which saves you from that frustrating "braid spinning on the arbor" problem.
- The drag is smooth and adjustable down to very light settings, making it a good match for 4–6 lb line and finicky fish.

**Con:**
- The anti-reverse can develop a slight click or slip over time if you're hard on it, and the reel isn't sealed, so it's strictly a freshwater tool.

**Real-world use:** I've fished Presidents for walleye on the Detroit River and for smallmouth on rocky northern lakes, and the smooth drag shines when a fish makes a short run near the boat. It's also a popular ice-fishing reel in the smaller sizes because it handles light line and cold temps reasonably well. For the money, few reels feel this refined.

**Best for:** Freshwater anglers who prioritize smoothness and a premium feel over saltwater durability.

## FAQ

**Q: What size spinning reel should I buy for bass fishing?**
A: For most bass fishing, a 2500 or 3000 size reel is the sweet spot. It balances well on a 6'6" to 7' medium or medium-heavy rod, holds enough 8–12 lb line, and has enough drag for typical bass. If you're throwing big swimbaits or fishing heavy cover, a 3000 or 4000 gives you more line capacity and drag.

**Q: Is a $50 spinning reel good enough, or should I spend more?**
A: A $50 reel like the Daiwa Revros LT or Pflueger President is absolutely good enough for most freshwater fishing. You're giving up some sealing, some drag strength, and some long-term durability compared to a $150 reel, but for bass, panfish, and walleye, a good $50–$80 reel will serve you for years if you maintain it.

**Q: How often should I service my spinning reel?**
A: For freshwater use, a full cleaning and re-greasing once a year is plenty, plus a rinse with fresh water after every trip if you fish dirty or brackish water. For saltwater, rinse after every trip and service the reel at least twice a year — salt is the number one killer of cheap reels.

**Q: Can I use a freshwater spinning reel in saltwater?**
A: You can, but you shouldn't expect it to last unless you rinse it thoroughly after every trip and re-grease it often. Reels like the Penn Pursuit IV are built with sealed drags and corrosion-resistant bearings specifically for saltwater, so if you fish the coast regularly, spend the extra few dollars and get a salt-ready reel.

**Q: What's more important, the reel or the rod?**
A: Both matter, but for most anglers the rod affects your fishing more day-to-day — it determines how you cast, how you feel bites, and how you fight fish. That said, a reel that binds up or has a jerky drag will ruin a good rod, so don't neglect the reel either. A balanced combo with a $70 reel and a $100 rod will out-fish a $200 reel on a $30 rod every time.
