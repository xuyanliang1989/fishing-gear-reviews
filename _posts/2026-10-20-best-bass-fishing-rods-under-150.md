## What to look for

Before you spend a dollar, focus on four things that separate a rod you'll keep for years from one that ends up in the garage sale pile.

- **Blank material and action.** Look for 30-ton to 36-ton graphite for a good balance of sensitivity and durability. A fast or extra-fast action gives you hook-setting power, but a moderate action loads better for crankbaits and treble-hook lures.
- **Guide quality.** Stainless steel frames with ceramic inserts (aluminum oxide at minimum, SiC or zirconia if the budget allows) resist grooving from braid and cast smoother.
- **Handle and reel seat.** EVA foam is durable and grippy; cork is lighter and more sensitive but wears faster. A good reel seat (Fuji or equivalent) keeps the reel locked down under load.
- **Length and power for your technique.** 6'6"–7'0" medium-heavy is the all-around bass workhorse; go longer (7'2"–7'6") for flipping and pitching, shorter for finesse and skipping docks.

## FAQ

**What rod power is best for bass fishing under $150?**
A medium-heavy, fast-action rod is the most versatile choice for bass, handling jigs, Texas rigs, and moving baits reasonably well. If you mostly throw crankbaits, look for a medium or medium-heavy with a moderate action instead.

**Is a $150 rod really better than a $50 rod?**
In most cases, yes. The jump from entry-level to the $100–$150 range usually means better graphite (higher modulus), better guides, and a more sensitive blank — all of which translate to feeling more bites and setting hooks more reliably.

**Should I spend more on the rod or the reel?**
For most bass fishing, a balanced combo matters more than maxing out either component. If you're on a tight budget, a solid $100 rod paired with a $70–$100 reel will outperform a $150 reel on a $50 rod.

**What length rod is best for bass fishing from a boat?**
A 7'0" to 7'3" rod is a great all-around length for boat fishing — long enough for casting distance and leverage, short enough to manage around the deck. Go shorter (6'6"–6'10") for skipping docks and tight cover.

**Can I use a spinning rod for bass fishing?**
Absolutely. Spinning rods excel at finesse techniques like drop-shotting, shaky heads, and weightless soft plastics, and they're easier for beginners to cast accurately. Many bass anglers carry both spinning and casting setups.
